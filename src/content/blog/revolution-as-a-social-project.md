In her 1977 film _De Cierta Manera_, pioneering Afro-Cuban filmmaker Sara Gómez rejects mainstream cinematic realities in favour of a then-emerging third cinema (Davis et al. 111). The film blends the fictional relationship of Yolanda, a privileged teacher, and Mario, a typical macho labourer, as they work to confront their respective cultural biases—both for the sake of themselves and Cuba itself. In the film’s final segment, we begin with Cuban labourers debating in the aftermath of a workers tribunal, before moving into a montage sequence where Mario and Yolanda bicker over scenes of marginal communities, arguing as they walk together through the city (_De Cierta Manera_ 1:07:44-1:14:36). Through its blend of documentary and fiction, a montage setting progress against persisting marginalization, and an open, unresolved ending, _De Cierta Manera_’s final sequence posits revolution not as a completed state initiative, but as a continuous, ongoing social process.

The sequence begins with a longer (just over three minute-long) scene in a single shot, situating us with a group of labourers arguing in the aftermath of a workers tribunal, where Mario exposed his friend Humberto’s idleness (_De Cierta Manera_ 1:07:44-1:10:48). Here, the cinematographic mode maps closely to the observational documentary, acting as a fly on the wall, shooting handheld with the lightweight 16mm camera commonplace in the movement (Davis et al. 66). 
![](@assets/20261020-132435.png "Figure 1")These borrowed aesthetics, combined with the practical, ambient lighting illuminating the workers’ grimy figures and costumes (see Figure 1), lend an air of reality to the mostly-scripted sequence. Whilst Mario’s exposure of Humberto is fiction, the vital importance of the depicted epiphany is anything but. There’s a distinct moral ambiguity here; the other labourers argue and disagree about Mario’s actions, questioning his fulfillment of his different, conflicting identities. Did he do well as a man? As a worker? As a revolutionary? These are questions Mario himself is still struggling with until the film’s end. In presenting these unresolved dilemmas, Gómez consciously departs from the idealized, heroic archetypes of socialist realism, choosing instead to portray “the collision between theory and practice—between the revolutionary program and the real difficulty of its implementation” (Alekseyeva 189).

As the debate reaches a head, a sudden smash cut shifts us into a dynamic montage, jump-started by the return of a recurrent visual motif: a wrecking ball crane demolishing old buildings in the slums of Havana (_De Cierta Manera_ 1:10:49; Figure 2). Just as the hard cut creates a collision between the long take’s intimacy and the ensuing montage’s momentum, the collision of the arguing labourers with the destructive image of the crane produces its own meaning: destroying the old to bring about the new (Davis et al. 254). 
![](@assets/20261020-132933.png "Figure 2")The film then turns to real locations and people amidst the slums of Havana—slums that persist in spite of state development (_De Cierta Manera_ 1:12:10-1:12:38). Our central pair are reintroduced in the midst of this montage, costumed and lit no differently than anyone else, ever in overcast lighting and dressed in common clothes. As Nichols notes, this formal use of parallel editing allows the viewer to connect separate stories or lines of action (140). Here, the viewer can directly relate Mario and Yolanda’s individual conflicts to the broader sociocultural story of Cuba at the time. By intercutting their fictional struggle with Cuba’s nonfictional one, and foregoing standard continuity cues, the montage creates a unified social world (Davis et al. 250), inextricably tying Mario and Yolanda’s personal conflict to the larger sociopolitical landscape of 1974 Havana. In refusing to delineate which parts, characters, and events of its narrative are fictional, _De Cierta Manera_ pushes the viewer to themselves do the “work of reconciling multiple ‘truths’”, and in so doing confront Yolanda, Mario, and their own constructed worldviews (Alekseyeva 171).

As the montage ensues, and Yolanda and Mario argue, only we don’t hear them. We hear only the non-diegetic repetition of “Véndele”, a song about needing to leave behind the world that “doesn’t care” and has “only bars to show its power” (_De Cierta Manera_ 1:11:19-1:11:34). We watch Mario and Yolanda quarrel, but the song speaks in their place, turning their private dispute into a social one. The song is itself written and sung by Guillermo Diaz, a real person inserted as himself into the fiction of Yolanda and Mario, blurring the staged and real once more (Alekseyeva 187). 
![](@assets/20261020-133839.png "Figure 3")The song’s call is that of the film itself—and yet, in the words of film critic Ruby Rich—“a house can be built faster than a person can change” (qtd. in Alekseyeva 185). As stated in the film’s own opening narration, resistant and antisocial attitudes persist ever still, even as conditions radically transform (_De Cierta Manera_ 0:09:27-0:09:42; 0:10:15-0:10:22). Mario’s misogynistic machismo and Yolanda’s classist privilege are segregational prisons they are learning to shed for the sake of their relationship and solidarity. As the song reaches crescendo, the camera gently zooms out and tilts upward as the pair continue to bicker and head off into the Miraflores neighbourhood, an image of completed—if only material—progress (_De Cierta Manera_ 1:12:38-1:13:11; Figure 3). The narrative then ends, without resolution; an open ending typical of art cinema (Nichols 139). Just as the revolution cannot be neatly tied up with a bow, neither can Mario or Yolanda’s stories or conflict.

Whilst _De Cierta Manera_’s final sequence illustrates the slow, messy material nature of real-world progress, it nonetheless also shows it to be profoundly possible. Whilst they bicker, Yolanda and Mario still walk off together. Formally, Gómez combines explosive motifs of physical change with observational, documentarian aesthetics, grounding her claims on the inadequacy of structural reform alone. Through a parallel montage that intercuts documentary footage of the Havana slums with its bickering central couple, the film rejects classical Hollywood continuity, refusing to relegate the spectator to a passive observer. Simultaneously, by rejecting socialist realism, Gómez exposes underlying societal inadequacies through imperfect workers whose identities and worldviews remain in flux. Withholding a neat narrative resolution, _De Cierta Manera_ argues this revolution is a living process. Even as credits roll, repeated sounds of construction and demolition continue to play (_De Cierta Manera_ 1:13:38)—a final reminder that the internal “revolution of the heart” is never truly complete (qtd. in Alekseyeva 189).
